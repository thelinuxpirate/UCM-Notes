* Chapter 7 | Thinking & Intelligence
** Cognition
Definition: Cognition refers to all mental activities associated with acquiring, storing, retrieving, & using knowledge

It allows us to make decisions, learn from experience, & adapt to new situations
Cognitive psychology is central to understanding intelligence, learning, mental disorders, & human development

Includes processes:
- Perception
- Attention
- Memory
- Language
- Problem-solving
- Decision-making
*** Key Aspects
- Concept Formation: Grouping information into categories or schemas to understand the world
- Prototypes: The most typical example of a concept
- Cognitive Processes: These help us interpret, analyze, & respond to environmental stimuli

** Concepts & Prototypes
Concepts are mental groupings of similar objects, ideas, or experiences
They help us organize information & simplify our thinking

 Concepts & prototypes allow us to process information efficiently, but they can also lead to
 biases or errors when something doesn’t match our prototype (e.g., not recognizing that a bat is a mammal)
*** Prototype Theory
A prototypeis the most typical or average example of a concept
Prototypes are influenced by culture, experience, & exposure

** Problem Solving
Process of finding solutionsto difficult or complex issues
Psyc Definition: Involves identifying a goal & taking steps to achieve it

Awareness of cognitive biases & flexibility in thinking improves problem-solving ability
*** Strategies
- Trial & Error: Trying different solutions until something works
  Pros: May work when options are limited
  Cons: Time-consuming & inefficient for complex problems

- Algorithm: A step-by-step, logical rule or procedurethat guarantees a solution
  Pros: Accurate, reliable
  Cons: Can be slow, not always practical in real life

- Heuristic: A mental shortcut or “rule of thumb” that helps simplify decision-making
  Pros: Fast, efficient
  Cons: Increases risk of errors & biases

- Insight: A sudden realization of a problem’s solution, often called an “Aha!” moment
  Often occurs unconsciouslyor after stepping away from the problem

*** Obstacles
- Mental Set:           Tendency to approach problemsin a familiar way, even when a new way might be better
- Functional Fixedness: Inability to see an object as useful for something other than its traditional use

** Decision Making & Biases
Definition: The process of choosing between alternatives based on preferences, information, & possible outcomes

Involves evaluating evidence, predicting consequences, & selecting a course of action
Often uses heuristics, which can be efficient; but may also introduce biases

Understanding viases helps us become more self-aware, rational thinkers
*** Decision-Making Heuristics
- Availability Heuristic: Making decisions based on how easily information comes to mind
  Cons: Can distort risk perception

- Representativeness Heuristic: Judging a situation based on how similar it is to a prototype
  Cons: Can lead to sterotyping
*** Cognitive Biases in Decision Making
- Conformation Bias:   Tendency to seek out or interpret informationin a way that confirms our existing beliefs
- Anchoring Bias:      Relying too heavily on the first piece of information encountered (the “anchor”)
- Hindsight Bias:      Belief that we "knew it all along" after an outcome is known
- Overconfidence Bias: Overestimating our own knowledge or ability

** Creativity & Divergent Thinking
Creativity Definition: The ability to generate, create, or discover new ideas, solutions, & possibilities

Creativity is not easily capturedby standard IQ tests
Psychologists also assess creativity through creative achievement, such as artistic work, inventions, or problem-solving in real life

Divergent Thinking Definition: The process of exploring many possible solutions
- Encourages open-edned thinking
- Used in brainstorming or creative writing

Convergent Thinking:
- Narrowing down options to find the single best solution
- Used in most academic testing & logical problem-solving
*** Traits of Creative Thinkers
- Originality: Producing ideas that are novel or unique
- Fluency:     Ability to produce many ideas
- Flexibility: Thinking in vaired ways or form different perspectives
- Elaboration: Building on an idea by adding detail or complexity
*** Fostering Creativity
- Environmental support:               Encouragement,open-minded peers, & freedom to explore
- Risk-taking & tolerance for failure: Accepting trial-&-error processes
- Curiosity & intrinsic motivation:    Enjoying the process, not just the outcome
- Cross-disciplinary learning:         Drawing from multiple knowledge areas
*** Characteristics of Creative Thinkers
- Open to new experiecnes
- Willing to take risks
- Comfortable with ambiguity (not everything being exact)
- Persistent in the face of obstacles
*** Barriers to Creativity
- Functional fixedness: Inability to see an object being used in a way other than its typical use
- Mental set:           Tendency to approach problems using strategies that have worked in the past—even
                        when they’re no longer effective
- Confirmation bias:    Only seeking evidence that supports our current beliefs
- Fear of failure or social judgment
** Intelligence & IQ Testing
General Definition: The ability to acquire & apply knowledge & skills
Encompasses reasoning, problem-solving, learning, memory, & adapting to new situations

Psychologists have debated whether intelligence is a single ability or multiple types of abilities
*** Theories of Intelligence
- Charles Spearman “g factor”:          People who perform well in one area (e.g., math) tend to perform well in others (e.g., language)

- Raymond Cattell - Divided intelligence into:
  "Fluid Intelligence":        Ability to solve new problems without prior knowledge (peaks in young adulthood)
  "Crystallized Intelligence": Accumulated knowledge & verbal skills (grows with age)

- Robert Sternberg - Triarchic Theory (3 types of intelligence):
  Analytical (problem-solving, school-based tasks)
  Creative   (innovative thinking)
  Practical  (“street smarts”)
**** Theory of Multiple Intelligences - Howard Gardner
- Linguistic:           Sensitivity to spoken & written language (e.g., writers, lawyers)
- Logical-Mathematical: Ability to analyze problems logically & perform mathematical operations (e.g., scientists, engineers)
- Musical:              Skill in performance, composition, & appreciation of musical patterns
- Bodily-Kinesthetic:   Ability to use one’s body to express oneself or solve problems (e.g., dancers, athletes)
- Spatial:              Capacity to think in three dimensions (e.g., architects, artists)
- Interpersonal:        Understanding & interacting effectively with others (e.g., teachers, therapists)
- Intrapersonal:        Understanding one’s own emotions & motivations
- Naturalistic:         Recognizing & categorizing aspects of the natural world (e.g., biologists, farmers)

Gardner argued that traditional education overemphasizes linguistic & logical-mathematical intelligence while undervaluing others

*** Intelligence Testing & Assessment
Intelligence testing aims to measure a person's cognitive abilities, such as reasoning, problem-solving, & understanding
These tests attempt to provide a quantitative measure of intelligence, often summarized as an IQ (Intelligence Quotient) score
Good IQ tests are both reliable & valid, but still face criticism

Intelligence is dynamic, influenced by environment, & not fully determined by genetics
Important to consider multiple measures & context when evaluating intellectual ability

Average IQ:
- Average IQ = 100
- Most people = 85-115
- 70 = Mental Disablity
- 130 = Super Genius

Alfred Binet (early 1900s, France):
- Developed one of the first intelligence tests to identify children needing academic support
- Introduced concept of mental age (the level of performance typically associated with a certain chronological age)

Lewis Terman (Stanford University):
- Adapted Binet’s test for American use: the Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scale
- Coined the term IQ = (Mental Age/Chronological Age) * 100

Key Takeaway:
IQ tests can provide useful information about cognitive abilities, but they are not a complete measure of intelligence
They should be interpreted cautiously & used alongside other forms of assessment
**** Common Tests
- Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scales
- Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS)
- Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC)
**** Critisisms
- Uses: Educational placement, identifying intellectual disabilities or giftedness, cognitive research
- Limitations:
  May not capture all forms of intelligence
  Can be culturally biased or influenced by socioeconomic background
  Overemphasis on test scores can ignore creativity, emotional intelligence, & real-world problem solving

** Emotional Intelligence & Social Intelligence
Emotional Intelligence (EI) Definition: The capacity to recognize, understand, manage, & use emotions effectively in ourselves & others
Popularized by Daniel Goleman, who argued it can be as important as traditional IQ inpredicting success

Intelligence is multi-dimensional: Cognitive abilities, emotional skills, & social awareness all contribute to success (against Golman)

Importance:
Predicts academic & workplace performance, social success, & mental health

Strong EI is associated with:
- Better relationship satisfaction
- Greater resilience & stress management
- Improved leadership & teamwork

Social Intelligence (SI) Definition: The ability to navigate complex social environments & build positive relationships

Involves:
- Reading social cuesaccurately (e.g., body language, tone of voice)
- Adjusting behaviorto fit different social situations
- Understanding group dynamics & unspoken social rules
*** EI Vs. SI
- Social intelligence emphasizes interpersonal navigation
- Emotional intelligence includes self-regulationas well
*** Key Components EI - Goleman’s Model
- Self-Awareness: Recognizing your own emotions & their effects
                  Understanding how mood influecnes behavior & though

- Self-Regulation: Managing impluses, mood, & behaviors
                   Thinking before acting; maintaining emotional control

- Motivation: Using emotions to pursue goals with energy & persistence
              Delaying gratification & staying optimistic

- Empathy: Understanding others’ feelings & perspectives
           Essential for healthy relationships & social understanding

- Social Skills: Managing relationships, conflict resolution, effective communication
                 Building rapport & leadership skills
*** Applications in Real Life
- Education
- Workplace
- Mental Health
** Points
- Intelligence is multi-faceted
- Intelligence is influenced by biology, experience, & culture
- Emotional intelligence highlights the role of understanding & managing emotions
- Measuring intelligence: IQ tests aim to assess mental ability but can be influenced by
  environment, education, & cultural context
* Chapter 11 | Personality Psychology
Personality Definition: Refers to the characteristic thoughts, emotional responses, & behaviors
                        that are relatively stable over time & across situations

It captures what makes individuals unique & how they tend to consistently think, feel, & act

Why it matters:
- Helps us understand & predict behavior
- Influences interpersonal relationships, academic & career success, & mental health
- Plays a role in how we perceive the world, make decisions, & interact with others

Features:
- Consistency:            Personality traits are generally consistent over time
- Individual Differences: No two people have the EXACT SAME personality; even identical twins are different
- Integration:            Personality brings together a person's motivations, emotions, values,
                          & patterns of behavior into a coherent whole
** Historical Theories of Personality
*** Psychodynamic Theory - Sigmund Freud
Core Idea: Personality is influenced by unconscious drives, inner conflicts, & childhood experiences

Structure of the Mind:
- Id:       Primitive desires (pleasure principle)
- Ego:      Rational self (reality principle)
- Superego: Internalized morals (conscience)

- Defense Mechanisms:  Unconscious tactics to reduce anxiety (e.g., repression, denial,projection)
- Psychosexual Stages: Personality develops through stages (oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital); each focused on different erogenous zones
**** Psychoanalytic Theory of Personality - Frued
Core Idea:
- Freud believed that unconscious forces—thoughts, memories, & desires—shapepersonality & behavior.
- Developed in the early 1900s, this was one of the first formal theories of personality in psychology.
***** The Three Personality Structures & Defense Mechanisms:
1. 
- Id(unconscious): Present from birth. 
- Operates on the pleasure principle—seeks immediate gratification.
- Primitive, instinctual drives (e.g., hunger, sex, aggression).
2. 
Ego (conscious & unconscious): Develops during childhood
- Operates on the reality principle—balances id’s desires with real-world constraints
- Acts as the rational mediator between the id & superego.
3.
Superego (mostly conscious): Develops last, around age 5
- Internalized societal rules & moral standards (conscience)
- Strives for perfection & judges our behavior.

Defense Mechanisms (Used by the ego to reduce anxiety):
- Repression:   Pushing unacceptable thoughts into the unconscious
- Denial:       Refusing to acknowledge reality
- Projection:   Attributing one’s own unacceptable desires to others
- Displacement: Redirecting emotions to a safer outlet
- Sublimation:  Channeling impulses into socially acceptable behaviors
***** Contributions
- Introduced the idea of the unconscious mind
- Emphasized the role of early childhood experiences
- Influenced the development of talk therapy & later psychodynamic approaches
***** Critisisms
- Lacks scientific rigor many concepts are not testable or falsifiable
- Overemphasis on sexual & aggressive drives
- Based primarily on case studies of a narrow population (wealthy European patients)
**** Freud’s Psychosexual Stages of Development
Freud proposed that personality develops through a series of stages centered on different erogenous zones
Each stage involves a conflict that must be resolved; unresolved conflictsmay lead to fixation; a lasting focus on that stage
Development is largely complete by early childhood

Stages:
- Oral (0-1): Mouth; Weaning; Smoking, dependecy issues
- Anal (1-3): Anus; Toilet Training; Obsessiveness or messiness
- Phallic (3-6): Genitals; Oedipus/Electra Complex; Sexual dysfunction, vanity
- Latency (6-12): None (dormant); Social Development; Poor peer relationships
- Genital (12+): Genitals; Mature sexuality; Unstable relationships
***** The Mind’s Structure: The Iceberg Analogy
- Conscious: Thoughts & feelings we’recurrently aware of
- Preconscious: Easily accessible memories or knowledge (just beneath the surface)
- Unconscious: Deep desires, fears, & memories influencing us without our awareness
*** Neo-Freudians
Built on Freud’s ideas but placed less emphasis on sex & more on social & cultural influences

- Carl Jung: Emphasized the collective unconscious & archetypes; introduced ideas like introversion & extraversion
- Alfred Adler: Focused on inferiority complex & drive for superiority
- Karen Horney: Criticized Freud’s views on women; emphasized anxiety, security, & social relationships
**** Neo-Freudian Theories
Definition: Neo-Freudians were psychologists who agreed with Freud’s ideas about the unconscious mind
            & childhood importance, but they diverged from his emphasis on sexual drives & focused more on
            social, cultural, & interpersonal influences

***** Carl Jung
- Collective Unconscious: A shared, inherited reservoir of memory traces from our species’ history
  - Contains archetypes: universal themes & symbols (e.g., the Hero, the Mother, theShadow
- Viewed personality development as a lifelong journey toward self-realization, or individuation
- Introduced terms like introversion & extraversion
***** Alfred Adler
- Emphasized the importance of social connections, rather than sexual motivations
- Introduced the concept of the inferiority complex:
  - Feelings of inferiority can drive individuals to strive for superiority & success
- Stressed the role of birth orderin shaping personality:
  - Firstborns, middle children, & youngest siblings may develop different traits due to family dynamics.
***** Erik Erikson
- Developed the psychosocial stages of development:
  - Unlike Freud’s psychosexual model, Erikson proposed 8 stagesacross the lifespan
  - Each stage involves a central conflict (e.g., trust vs. mistrust, identity vs. role confusion)
- Emphasized lifelong development & social relationships
***** Karen Horney
- One of the first female psychoanalysts to challenge Freud’s ideas
- Rejected Freud’s concept of “penis envy”, proposing instead that men may suffer from “womb envy”
- Emphasized the sociocultural context of personality, especially the impact of anxiety & interpersonal relationships
- Highlighted the importance of culture & gender roles in shaping personality
*** Humanistic Theories
Emphasize free will, self-awareness, & the drive toward personal growth

Carl Rogers:
- Concept of self-concept: How you see yourself vs. your ideal self.
- Believed in unconditional positive regardas necessary for healthy development

Abraham Maslow:
- Proposed the Hierarchy of Needsculminating in self-actualization.
- Saw personality as shaped by striving toward personal fulfillment
**** Humanistic Approaches
Developed as a reaction to the pessimism of Freud’s psychoanalytic theory & the mechanical view of behaviorism

Emphasizes:
- Personal growth
- Self-awareness
- Free will
- The inherent goodness of people

Seeks to understand how people can achieve fulfillment & self-actualization
***** Person-Centered Theory - Carl Rogers (1902–1987)
Self-Concept:
- How we view ourselves (e.g., “I am capable,” “I am kind”)
- Personality is shaped by the alignment (or misalignment) between our self-concept & experience
- Healthy personality = a congruent self-concept

Unconditional Positive Regard:
- Accepting & valuing a person without judgment
- Children raised with unconditional positive regard develop a more accurate & confident self-concept
- Contrasts with conditional positive regard, where acceptance depends on meeting others' expectations

Therapeutic Application:
- Rogers emphasized a non-directive approach where the therapist offers empathy, genuineness, & acceptance
- Helped develop client-centered therapy, a foundational approach in counseling psychology
***** Hierarchy of Needs - Abraham Maslow (1908–1970)
Founder of the Pyramid Model
- Physiological:      Food, water, sleep
- Safety:             Security, health, stability
- Love/Belonging:     Relationships, social connection
- Esteem:             Self-worth, confidence, recognition
- Self-Actualization: Reaching one’s full potential, creativity, purpose

People are motivated to satisfy needs in order, starting from the most basic

People who reach Self-Actualization:
- Motivated by growth rather than fear
*** Hippocrates (ca. 460–370 BCE)
Believed personality was based on the balance of four bodily fluids or “humors”:
- Blood -> Sanguine (cheerful, optimistic)
- Yellow bile -> Choleric (irritable, ambitious)
- Black bile -> Melancholic (sad, thoughtful)
- Phlegm -> Phlegmatic (calm, reliable)

Illness & personality imbalances were thought to arise from excesses or deficiencies in these fluids
*** Galen (129–c. 200 CE)
Expanded on Hippocrates’ theory by proposing that the four humors produced specific temperaments
Suggested that temperament influences both health & personality traits
Though outdated, these ideas influenced personality theories for centuries
*** Phrenology - Franz Gall (1758–1828)
Gall believed the shape & bumps of the skullreflected a person’s mental faculties & personality traits
He mapped the skull into specific regionsthought to correspond to traits like combativeness, kindness, or wit
Phrenology is now discredited, but it was one of the earliest attempts to link biology to personality
*** Historical Importance
These theories were foundational in shaping how psychologists think about:
- The originof personality
- The role of early experiences
- The importance of the self & conscious choice

While many ideas are now outdated or unsupported, they influenced modern theories & clinical practices
*** Legacy of Early Theories
Though not scientifically valid, these early ideas:
- Represent the origins of biological & typological thinkingabout personality
- Influenced the development of more rigorous psychological models
- Laid the groundwork for trait theory & biological perspectives
** Learning & Behaviorist Approaches to Personality
*** Personality as Learned Behavior - B.F. Skinner
Personality is shaped by the environment, not by internal traits orunconscious forces.
Skinner rejected introspection & focused on observable behavior

Operant Conditioning:
- Behavior is strengthened through reinforcement(positive/negative)
- Behavior is weakened through punishment

No emphasis on internal traits: Behavior patterns that appear stable are really a result of consistent reinforcement histories
*** Social Cognitive Theory - Albert Bandura
Reciprocal Determinism:
- Personality arises from the interaction of behavior, environment, & cognitive factors
- You influence your environment, & your environment influences you
Example: Choosing to play a sport (behavior) affects your environment (team), which shapes your self-concept & future decisions

Self-Efficacy:
- One’s belief in their ability to succeed in specific situations
- High self-efficacy -> more motivation & resilience
- Low self-efficacy -> more likely to give up or avoid challenges

Observational Learning:
- Learning by watching others
- Influences personality development, especially in childhood
Example: Children may adopt behaviors they see in parents, peers, or media figures
*** Locus of Control - Julian Rotter
Definition: A person’s belief about what causes outcomes in their life

Internal Locus of Control:
- Belief that outcomes are the result of personal effort
- Associated with higher achievement, better health, & greater responsibility

External Locus of Control:
- Belief that outcomes are due to luck, fate, or external circumstances
- Can lead to passivity or a sense of helplessness

Personality is influenced by where people place control over life’s events
**** Main Points
- Behaviorist theories emphasize learning & environment, not innate traits or unconscious processes
- Social cognitive theories integrate thought processes (like beliefs & expectations) with behavior & social context
- These theories have strong applications in education, therapy, motivation, & understanding behavior in real-life settings

** Trait Theories of Personality
Trait theorists focus on identifying & describing stable, measurable personality traits that differ between individuals
Form the foundation for many modern personality assessments(e.g., Big Five model, MBTI is loosely inspired)
Help predict behavior patterns, job performance, & interpersonal compatibility

Trait theory is descriptive but doesn’t fully explain why traits develop; making it complementary to other theories
*** Trait Hierarchies - Gordon Allport (1897–1967)
Proposed that individuals have unique combinationsof traits

3 Levels of Traits:
- Cardinal Traits: Rare, dominate an individual’s entire personality (e.g., Mother Teresa’s altruism)
- Central Traits: General characteristics found in varying degrees in everyone (e.g., honesty, friendliness)
- Secondary Traits: More situation-specific & less consistent (e.g., preferences or attitudes)

Believed personality was shaped by both genetics & environment, but emphasized theindividual’s uniqueness
*** 16 Personality Factors - Raymond Cattell (1905–1998)
- Used factor analysisto reduce Allport’s extensive list of traits
- Identified 16 source traits, each lying on a continuum (e.g., Reserved vs. Outgoing)
- Developed the 16PF Questionnaire, which is still used in psychological assessment,including career counseling & clinical settings
- Viewed traits as underlying structuresthat influence behavior, not just surface-level observations
*** 3-Factor Model - Hans & Sybil Eysenck
Believed personality could be boiled down to three dimensions:

- Introversion vs. Extraversion

- Neuroticism vs. Stability
  - Neurotic: Anxious, moody, prone to negative emotions
  - Stable: Calm, even-tempered, emotionally steady

- Psychoticism
  - Linked to aggressiveness, creativity, impulsivity, & a detachment from reality

This 3-Factor Model Emphasized the biological basis of traits;
- Claimed traits had a genetic & physiological origin, such as arousal levels in the brain
*** The 5-Factor Model
AKA Big Five, the Five-Factor Model is the most widely accepted & researched model of personality traits in modern psychology
- Developed through decades of factor analysison trait descriptors
- The model is empirically supportedacross cultures & age groups

The Big Five provides a balanced, evidence-based frameworkf or understanding personality
It is non-pathological (not based on disorders) & universally applicableacross cultures & languages
**** OCEAN Traits
Each trait is measured on a spectrum; individuals fall somewhere between the two extremes of each dimension.

- Openness to Experience:
  - High: Imaginative, curious, creative, open to new experiences & unconventional ideas
  - Low: Practical, routine-oriented, prefers familiarity over novelty

- Conscientiousness:
  - High: Organized, dependable, disciplined, goal-directed
  - Low: Careless, impulsive, disorganized, easily distracted

- Extraversion:

Longitudinal studiesshow these traits are relatively stable, especially after age 30
Openness & extraversion, may decline modestly over time

** Biological & Genetic Perspectives on Personality
*** Twin Studies & Heritability of Personality
Twin studiesare a key method for examining the role of genetics in personality
- Identical (monozygotic): Twins share 100% of their genes
- Fraternal (dizygotic):   Twins share ~50% of their genes, like any other siblings

By comparing personality similarities between these two types of twins, researchers estimate heritability
- Heritability is the proportion of trait variation attributed to genetic differences
- For many personality traits, heritability estimates range from 40% to 60%

Even when raised apart, identical twins tend to be more alikein personality than fraternal twins raised together
*** Temperament as a Biological Foundation
Temperament: Innate, biologically based tendencies to feel or act in certain ways
Common dimensions include:
- Activity level(how energetic a child is)
- Emotional reactivity (how easily upset or soothed)
- Sociability(willingness to engage with others)

Studies suggest that temperament remains relatively stable across the lifespan but can be shaped by environment
*** Brain Structure & Neurotransmitters
- Brain structure & function: For example, introverts may have a more sensitive reticular activating system (involved in arousal)
- Amygdala: Tied to emotional reactivity - linked to traits like neuroticism
- Prefrontal cortex: Involved in impulse control & planning—related to conscientiousness

Neurotransmitter systems also play a role:
- Dopamine:  Linked to reward sensitivity (high in extraverts)
- Serotonin: Influences mood regulation (low levels associated with high neuroticism).
*** Key Takeaways
- Nature matters:                  Biological factors clearly influence personality but they are not destiny
- Genes interact with environment: Traits are shaped by experiences, relationships, & culture across the lifespan
** Cultural Influences on Personality
*** Individualistic vs. Collectivistic Cultures
Individualistic Cultures: U.S, Canda, Europe
- Emphasizes independence
- People see themselves as unique & autonomous
- Personality Traits: Assertiveness, Self-Reliance, Personal Goals

Collectivistic Cultures: China, Japan, Korea, many Latin American & African countries
- Emphasize group harmony, family obligations, & interdependence
- People in these cultures tend to define themselves through their social roles & relationships
- Traits such as cooperativeness, respect, & group loyalty are highly valued

Cultural values can influence:
- How traits are perceived
- How traits are measured
- The social desirability of traits
*** Acculturation & Bicultural Identity
People who move between or live between different cultures (e.g., immigrants, international students) may develop a bicultural personality
- Acculturation: The process of adjusting to a new culture, which can affect personality expression over time

Research shows that personality is dynamic, not fixed; it can evolve as individuals adapt to different cultural expectations
*** Personality Assessment
Self-Report Inventories Definition: Standardized questionnaires where individuals report on their own behaviors, thoughts, & feelings
Projective Tests Definiton: Asses personality by presenting ambigous simili & interpeting responses on unconscious processes

MMPI (Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory):
- One of the most widely used psychological assessments

* Chapter 12 | Social Psychology
Social Psychology Definition: The scientific study of how individuals think, feel, & behave in social situations

Goals:
- Understand how others influence our behavior
- Explore how we perceive & judge others
- Examine how social contexts shape thoughts & emotions

Social psychology largely favors situationism
** Focus Areas
- Thoughts  (e.g., stereotypes, attributions)
- Feelings  (e.g., empathy, prejudice, attraction)
- Behaviors (e.g., conformity, helping, aggression)

Emphasis on the power of the situation & social context
** Why Study?
Ultimately, studying social psychology helps us navigate social life more wisely

Has practical applications in a range of careers:

- Healthcare:      Helps promote behavior change like quitting smoking or improving diet
- Education:       Key for understanding group dynamics & classroom behavior
- Business:        Drives consumer behavior & marketing strategies
- Law Enforcement: Important for understanding conformity, obedience, & eyewitness testimony

It gives us tools to become more:
- Empathetic
- Informed
- Socially responsible individuals
** Situationism vs. Dispositionism
- Situationism:   Behavior is influenced more by the environment & context
  Situationismis the idea that our behavior is primarily shaped by external factors—the environment,
  social norms, the presence of others

- Dispositionism: Behavior is due to internal traits, personality, or character
  Dispositionism attributes behavior to internal traits—like personality, attitudes, or values

Social psychology emphasizes: The power of the situation
** Fundamental Attribution Error (FAE)
FAE Definiton: Tendency to overemphasize dispositional factors & underestimate situational factors when explaining others’ behavior
- Assumes behavior reflects the person, not the context
- Is one of the most well-established findings in social psychology
- Refers to our tendency to assume that other people’s behavior reflects who they are
  their personality or character; rather than considering the situation they might be in

Cultures:
- Individualistic Cultures (U.S): Error more common
- Collectivistic Cultures:        People are more likely to factor in social roles & situations

Attribution:
- How we explain others’ behavior (e.g., fundamental attribution error, actor-observer bias)
- Influenced by culture & context
** Actor-Observer Bias & Self-Serving Bias
How we explain our own behavior versus others'

- Actor-Observer Bias: We explain our own behavior using situational factors,
  but others’ behavior using dispositional traits

The actor-observer bias occurs when we’re the actor, & we blame the situation for our behavior
but when we’re the observer, we blame the person.

- Self-Serving Bias: We attribute our successesto internal traits, & our failures to external factors

The self-serving bias helps us protect our self-esteem

- Both serve to protect self-esteem & reduce cognitive dissonance

** Cultural Influences on Attribution
Individualistic cultures (e.g., U.S., Canada, Western Europe):
- Emphasize personal responsibility, autonomy
- More likely to use dispositional attributions

Collectivistic cultures (e.g., Japan, China, Latin America):
- Emphasize group harmony, context, interdependence
- More likely to use situational attributions

These cultural differences affect how we interpret social interactions, assign blame, & offer praise
Being aware of them helps us develop more cultural sensitivity & empathy, especially in multicultural environments
** What Are Attitudes
Attitude Definition: Evaluation of a person, idea, or object

Three Components (ABC Model):
- Affective:  How we feel
- Behavioral: How we act
- Cognitive:  What we believe
- Formed through experience & socialization
Attitudes guide behavior but not always perfectly

*** Changing Attitudes - Persuasion
Persuasion & the Yale Attitude Change Approach:
- Persuasion: Process of changing beliefs or attitudes in response to communication

Yale Model (1950s): 3 Key factors influence persuasion
- Source:   Credibility, attractiveness
- Message:  Quality, clarity, emotional appeal
- Audience: Attention, intelligence, age, mood
Attitude change depends on interaction of all three
Emphasizes that attitude change is not just about what is said: It’s also about who says it & who hears it

- The Source of the message: More persuasive if the communicator is credible, trustworthy, or physically attractive

- The Message itself:        Clear, logical messages tend to work unless emotion is more compelling for the audience
                             Messages that present both sidesof an argument (when appropriate) are often more effective

- The Audience:              Some people are easier to persuade than others
                             Younger people, people in a good mood, or those with moderate intelligence tend to be more persuadable
**** Routes of Persuasion - Elaboration Likelihood Model
Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM): Explains how people are persuaded

Two Routes to Persuasion: Central vs. Peripheral

Attitude Change:
- Central Route:    Logic, facts, thoughtful consideration
                    Works best when audience is motivated & analytical

- Peripheral Route: Surface cues like attractiveness, emotion, celebrity
                    Works when audience is not deeply engaged

Different Routes: Different types of attitude change

The Central Routeinvolves deep processing:
- The audience carefully evaluates the message’s logic & evidence
- If persuaded this way, the attitude change tends to be lasting & resistant to counter-persuasion

The Peripheral Routerelies on superficial cues:
- The audience may be distracted, disinterested, or emotionally influenced

** Cognitive Dissonance
Cognitive Dissonance Definition: Psychological discomfort from holding conflicting thoughts, beliefs, or behaviors
                                 When Attitudes & Actions Clash


We’re motivated to reduce dissonance by:
- Changing our attitude
- Changing our behavior
- Rationalizing the inconsistency
*** Leon Festinger’s Classic Study (1959)
Participants were asked to perform a boring task & then lie to another person, saying it was fun
- Some were paid $1, others $20
- Those paid only $1 were more likely to change their attitude & say they actually enjoyed the task
- They had insufficient justification for lying, so they experienced more dissonance & adjusted their beliefs to reduce it
This theory explains a lot of real-world behavior; why we justify purchases, stay in bad relationships, or downplay harmful habits
** Conformity
Conformity Definiton: Changing behavior to match the group
*** Asch’s Line Study (1951)
- Participants gave wrong answers to match group
- 76% conformed at least once

Shows power of normative social influence
People conform even when they know the group is wrong

In the Asch Line Study, participants were placed in a group with actors. Everyone was shown a line &
then asked to match it to one of three comparison lines.
The answer was obvious but the actors all gave the wrong answer aloud

The real participant was seated last or second-to-last & heard everyone before them give the wrong answer.
Despite knowing the correct line, 76% of participants conformedat least once, giving the wrong answer to go along with the group.
This shows the power of normative social influence; we conform to fit in, avoid conflict, or feel accepted

Importantly, Asch found that if just one other personbroke from the group, conformity dropped dramatically.
This highlights how even a little support can empower dissent

** Obedience
Obedience Definition: Following orders from an authority figure
*** Milgram’s study (1961) | Obedience to Authority
Tested how far people would go in obeying authority
- Participants instructed to deliver shocks to a “learner”
- 65% delivered the maximum 450 volts, despite stress

Participants were told they were part of a learning experiment
They played the role of the 'teacher', administering what they believed were increasingly severe electric shocks
to a 'learner' (actually an actor) whenever they answered a question incorrectly

Despite hearing the learner’s (fake) screams & pleas, 65% of participants continued all the way to 450 volts,
labeled as 'XXX –Danger'. This shocked the scientific community—& the public.

The study showed how situational forces, like the presence of a lab coat & a formal setting, can override personal conscience


Ethical Issues:
- Deception:          Participants believed they were harming someone
- Emotional distress: Many were visibly upset or sweating
Milgram's study helped shape today’s ethical research standards

Shows power of authority & situational pressure
Raised major ethical questionsin psychology

** The Bystander Effect
- Bystander Effect:            Tendency to be less likely to help when others are present
- Kitty Genovese case (1964):  Reportedly 38 witnesses, no one intervened
  - The term gained widespread attention after the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York
  - Reports at the time said 38 people witnessed the attack & did nothing
- Diffusion of Responsibility: Each person assumes someone else will act

The key mechanism is diffusion of responsibility; "we assume someone else will do something, so we don’t have to."
The Bystander Effect: Occurs when people in a group fail to intervene, thinking others will step in.
The more people there are, the less personal responsibility each individual feels

Psychologists Latane & Darley followed up with lab experiments showing that when participants believed
they were alone, they were much more likely to help a person in distress.
But when they thought others were also aware, helping rates dropped sharply

Prosocial Behavior:
- Helping behaviors influenced by mood, similarity, modeling, & diffusion of responsibility
*** Factors That Promote Helping
What makes people more likely to help?
- Positive Mood: People are more generous when they feel good
- Gender:        Women more likely to receive help; men more likely to help in dangerous situations
- Similarity:    We help those who seem like us
- Modeling:      Seeing others help increases likelihood we will too

Sigmund Freud: Founder of Psychoanalysis | Emphasis on the Unconscious

* Top 10 Psychology Experiments You Should Know About
** Pavlov’s Classical Conditioning (1890s)
*** Dogs, Bells, & Salivation
Ivan Pavlov, a Russian physiologist, was originally studying digestive processesin dogs.

- Noticed dogs salivated not just when food was presented, but also when they heard footsteps or saw lab assistants
- Designed experiments pairing a neutral stimulus (bell) with fooduntil the bell alone triggered salivation

- Unconditioned Stimulus (UCS): Food - Naturally produces a response
- Unconditioned Response (UCR): Salivation to food - automatic, not learned
- Conditioned Stimulus (CS):    Bell — initially neutral, becomes associated with food
- Conditioned Response (CR):    Salivation to bell —learned response.

Why it matters:
- Demonstrated that learning can occur through association between stimuli
- Laid groundwork for behaviorism, influencing later researchers like Watson & Skinner

Applications:
- Therapy:     Systematic desensitization for phobias
- Advertising: Pairing products with positive stimuli
- Education:   Classroom cues for behavior management
** Watson & Rayner – Little Albert (1920)
*** Conditioning Fear in an Infant
John B. Watson & Rosalie Rayner wanted to test if emotional responses could be classically conditioned

Subject: “Little Albert,” ~9-month-old infant

Procedure:
- Showed Albert a white rat (neutral stimulus)        — No fear response
- Paired the rat with a loud, frightening noise (UCS) — Caused crying (UCR)
- After repeated pairings, Albert cried at the sight of the rat alone (CR)
- Fear generalized to similar objects: White rabbit, fur coat, even Santa Claus beard.
*** Ethical Issues
- No informed consent from the child’s mother (as defined by modern standards)
- Deliberately induced distress in an infant
- No attempt to decondition the fear before ending the study
- Sparked later debates that led to stricter APA ethical guidelines
*** Modern Understanding
- Demonstrated that phobias can be learned through classical conditioning
- Supports the use of exposure therapy & systematic desensitization to unlearn fears
- Modern research uses ethical simulations or observational methods instead of causing distress
** Skinner’s Operant Conditioning (1930s–1950s)
*** Rats, Pigeons, Reinforcement, Punishment
- B.F. Skinner built on Thorndike’s Law of Effect, studying how consequences shape behavior
- Used the Skinner Box—controlled environment with levers (rats) or pecking disks (pigeons)

Reinforcement increases behavior:
- Positive punishment–adding something unpleasant (shock)
- Negative punishment–removing something pleasant (withholding food)
**** Schedules of Reinforcement
- Fixed Ratio (FR):       Reward after set number of responses (e.g., FR5 → every 5 presses)
- Variable Ratio (VR):    Reward after unpredictable number of responses — produces highest response rate (e.g., slot machines)
- Fixed Interval (FI):    Reward after set time period (e.g., every 2 minutes)
- Variable Interval (VI): Reward after varying time intervals (e.g., checking email).

Applications:
- Education:        Token economies
- Behavior Therapy: Reinforcemnt plans for habit change
- Animal Training:  Step-by-step
** Bandura’s Bobo Doll (1961)
*** Social Learning Theory: Modeling Aggression
Albert Bandura tested whether children learn behaviors by observing & imitating others

Method:
- Preschool children watched an adult model behave aggressively toward an inflatable Bobo doll (hitting, kicking, yelling)
- Control group saw non-aggressive behavior
- Later, when given the chance to play, children who saw aggression were more likely to act aggressively themselves

**** Media Influences on Behavior
- Findings showed observational learning can occur without direct reinforcement
- Suggested that exposure to violence in media could influence children’s behavior patterns
**** Relevance Today
- Applied to debates about violent video games, social media trends, & influencer behavior
- Current research also looks at prosocial modeling — how observing kindness & cooperation can encourage positive actions
** Asch’s Conformity Experiment (1951)
*** Judgment Task & Group Pressure
Solomon Asch investigated whether people would conform to a group’s obviously incorrect judgment

Method:
- Participants sat with a group (confederates of the researcher)
- Asked to match the length of a line to three comparison lines
- Confederates intentionally gave wrong answers on certain trials
^ Results: ~75% of participants conformed at least once, even when the answer was clearly wrong
*** Factors Influencing Conformity
- Group size (3–5 confederates increases conformity)
- Unanimity (one dissenter dramatically reduces conformity)
- Confidence & task difficulty (harder tasks increase conformity)
- Personality & self-esteem levels
*** Cultural & Situational Considerations
- Collectivist cultures tend to show higher conformity rates than individualist cultures
- Situational pressures (desire to be liked, avoid conflict) play a big role
** Milgram Obedience Experiment (1963)
Key Setup:
- Participants assigned role of “Teacher”; confederate always played “Learner.”
- Task: “Teacher” reads word pairs; if “Learner” answers incorrectly, they deliver electric shocks of increasing intensity (15–450 volts)
- “Learner” (actor) never actually shocked—cries of pain were pre-recorded
- Authority figure (experimenter in lab coat) urged: “Please continue,” “The experiment requires that you continue.”

Surprising Results:
- 65%of participants delivered the maximum 450 volts despite apparent distress
- Many showed visible stress—sweating, trembling, nervous laughter — but continued under authority pressure

Ethical Controversy:
- Participants deceived about shocks & purpose of study
- Emotional distress during experiment
- Sparked debate over informed consentand participant protection

Safeguards Today:
- Institutional Review Boards (IRBs)
- Informed consent with right to withdraw at any time
- Debriefing to explain true purpose & ensure well-being

Applications:
- Explains how ordinary people can commit harmful acts under authority
- Links to real-world atrocities (e.g., war crimes, corporate scandals)
- Relevant to workplace hierarchy, law enforcement, & military ethics
** Zimbardo’s Stanford Prison Experiment (1971)
- Conducted by Philip Zimbardoat Stanford University
- Aimed to explore how social roles, authority, & situational forcesinfluence behavior
- Simulated prison in Stanford’s basement; volunteers randomly assigned to guardor prisonerroles.

Key Findings:
- Guards quickly became authoritarian, some engaging in psychological abuse
- Prisoners exhibited emotional distress, depression, & passive submission
- The situation escalated far faster & more severelythan anticipated

Early Termination:
- Planned for 2 weeks but stopped after only 6 daysdue to extreme behavior & harm to participants
- Zimbardo’s girlfriend, Christina Maslach, confronted him about the ethical issues, prompting the shutdown
*** Stanford Prison Experiment Timeline (Zimbardo, 1971)
*Day 0 - Setup*
- 24 male college students selected, randomly assigned to guardsor prisoners
- Basement of Stanford’s psychology building converted into mock prison

*Day 1 –Arrival & Role Adoption*
- Prisoners “arrested” at home; taken to mock prison
- Guards begin enforcing rules; first signs of role acceptance appear

*Day 2 –Early Tension*
- Guards start psychological tactics: intimidation, arbitrary punishments
- Prisoners show signs of stress & anxiety

*Day 3 – Escalation*
- Several prisoners have severe emotional reactions (crying, rage, withdrawal)
- Guards become more aggressive; power imbalance fully evident

*Day 5 – Zimbardo Steps In*
- Originally planned for 2 weeks, stopped after 6 daysdue to escalating harm
- Demonstrated how quickly ordinary people can adopt abusive rolesunder situational pressures

*Day 6 – Experiment Terminated*
- Originally planned for 2 weeks, stopped after 6 daysdue to escalating harm
- Demonstrated how quickly ordinary people can adopt abusive rolesunder situational pressures
**** Lessons Learned
- Demonstrated deindividuation: Loss of self-awareness & moral restraint in a group or role
- Showed how systemic power dynamicscan lead to abuse, even among “ordinary” people
- Suggested that situations, not just personalities, can strongly shape actions
**** Ethical Impact
Raised major concerns about participant welfare, informed consent, & lack of oversight

Contributed to modern ethics reformsin psychological research:
- Institutional Review Boards (IRBs)
- Strict informed consent requirements
- Right to withdraw without penalty
- Continuous monitoring of participant well-being
** Loftus & Palmer – Eyewitness Memory (1974)
- Elizabeth Loftus & John Palmer were interested in how memory can be alteredafter an event
- Eyewitness testimony is often treated as reliable in court, but research suggested that post-event information might distort recall

Method & Key Findings:
- Participants watched a video of a car accident
- Later, they were asked questions about what they saw—critical question
  “About how fast were the cars going when they ___ each other?”

- The verb varied: smashed, collided, bumped, hit, contacted
- Results: Stronger verbs like smashed produced higher speed estimatesand more false reports of seeing broken glass (even when there was none)
*** Core Concept – The Misinformation Effect
- Memory is reconstructive, not like a video replay
- Wording of a questioncan introduce new information, which gets integrated into a person’s memory
*** Implications for the Legal System
- Eyewitness testimony can be unintentionally shapedby police questioning, media coverage, or lawyers
- Mistaken identifications & false memories contribute to wrongful convictions
*** Modern Replications & Debates
- Replicated many times with various events (e.g., thefts, accidents)
- Ongoing debate: How far can false memories go? Can entire fabricated eventsbe implanted?
- Led to reforms in police interview techniques (e.g., cognitive interviewing).
*** Key Takeaways
- Our memories are malleableand shaped by language, suggestion, & post-event information
- Critical thinking & methodological safeguardsare essential in legal settings
** Harlow’s Monkey Love Experiment (1950s–60s)
Core Question:
- What matters more in attachment - the physical need for food or the emotional need for comfort?

Method:
- Harry Harlow separated infant rhesus monkeys from their mothers shortly after birth
- The monkeys were placed in cages with two surrogate “mothers”:
  - Wire mother:  Made of bare wire, provided milk from a bottle
  - Cloth mother: Covered in soft terry cloth, provided no milk
- Observed which “mother” the infant monkeys preferred & how they behaved in frightening situations.
*** Harlow's Monkeys
Key Findings:
- Monkeys clung to the cloth mother for most of the day, even though it didn’t provide food
- When frightened, they ran to the cloth mother for security before exploring again
- Physical comfort & security were more important for attachment than nourishment alone

Impact on Theory:
- Strongly influenced John Bowlby’s & Mary Ainsworth’s attachment theory
- Demonstrated that emotional bonds are rooted in comfort, warmth, & security — not just biological survival needs

Applications:
- Shaped modern child welfare policies, discouraging institutional care without emotional nurturing
- Informed social work, foster care, & adoption practices, emphasizing the importance of consistent caregivers

Ethical Considerations:
- The monkeys suffered lasting emotional & social damage, raising concerns about the harm caused by animal research
- Today, stricter ethical guidelines for animal research would likely prevent such experiments
*** Testing
Overview:
- Setup: Participants completed a deliberately boring task (turning wooden pegs)
- Afterward, they were asked to tell the next participant the task was interesting
- Paid either $1or $20 to do so

Key Findings:
- Those paid $1 reported enjoying the task more than those paid $20
- Why? Cognitive Dissonance Theory — holding two conflicting cognitions (“I lied” vs. “I’m an honest person”) creates discomfort
- With only $1, participants couldn’t justify the lie externally, so they changed their internal attitudeto believe the task was more enjoyable

Core Concepts:
- Justification of Effort: When rewards are small, people often convince themselves their actions were worthwhile
- Attitude Change:         Internal beliefs shift to align with behavior to reduce dissonance

Applications:
- Marketing & Advertising: Small incentives can foster stronger belief change than large ones
- Persuasion:              Commitment without large reward can produce long-lasting attitude change
- Self-Concept:            People seek internal consistency, sometimes bending beliefs to match past choices
** Ethical Evolution in Research
Background:
- Early psychology studies (e.g., Milgram, Zimbardo, Harlow) revealed major gaps in ethical protections
- Public & professional concern over participant welfare led to formalized safeguards.

APA Code of Ethics:
- Established by the American Psychological Association to guide professional conduct
  - Core principles:
    - Beneficence & Nonmaleficence – Maximize benefits, minimize harm
    - Fidelity & Responsibility – Maintain trust & uphold responsibilities to society
    - Integrity – Be honest, accurate, & truthful
    - Justice – Ensure equitable access to research benefits
- Respect for People’s Rights & Dignity–Safeguard privacy, confidentiality, & autonomy

Institutional Review Boards (IRBs):
- Independent committees reviewing research proposals involving humans
- Ensure studies meet ethical standards before approval
- Assess risk vs. benefit, recruitment fairness, & participant protection

Key Safeguards Today:
- Informed Consent: Participants receive complete info about purpose, procedures, risks, benefits, & right to withdraw
- Debriefing:       Especially important if deception is used; clarify the study’s purpose, restore trust, & address any misconceptions
- Minimal Harm:     No greater risk than participants would encounter in daily life; extra care for vulnerable populations

Why It Matters:
- Protects participants from exploitation or trauma
- Preserves public trust in psychological research
- Ensures research results are ethically defensible & socially responsible
*** Examples
APA Code of Ethics:
- A psychologist running a workplace stress study follows APA guidelines by ensuring participants’ responses remain
  confidential & reporting results honestly without fabricating data
- A clinical trial on new depression treatments ensures that participants understand their right to withdraw at any time without penalty

Institutional Review Boards (IRBs):
- A university researcher submits their plan for a sleep deprivation study to the IRB, which requires adjustments to limit potential health risks
- A nonprofit proposing a survey on trauma survivors has to receive IRB approval to ensure the questions don’t cause undue psychological harm

Informed Consent:
- Before joining a mindfulness app effectiveness study, participants receive a clear document explaining what data will be collected,
  how it will be used, & any possible side effects.
- In a memory experiment, participants are told the tasks may involve mildly stressful
  timed tests so they can make an informed decision to participate

Debriefing:
- After a staged “lost wallet” experiment, researchers explain to participants that the event was set up to measure prosocial behavior
  & reassure them that no one was in distress.
- In a study involving misleading instructions, participants are told the true purpose afterward & given resources to discuss any discomfort they felt

Minimal Harm:
- A virtual reality fear experiment uses realistic but safe spider simulations rather than actual spiders for participants with arachnophobia
- A study on caffeine effects ensures participants are screened for heart conditions & caffeine sensitivity to avoid adverse reactions
